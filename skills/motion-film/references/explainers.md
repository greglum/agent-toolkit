# Explainer films

Read this when the film exists to teach one idea: a concept, mechanism, or process explainer.
It adds a teaching plan to the scene contract. Product journeys, brand, and renderer rules
still come from the owner and the main procedure.

## Discovery

- Write the one question the film answers in the viewer's words, and the one understanding
  they leave with. If either takes two sentences, the scope is still too wide.
- Record what the audience already believes. The strongest hook is usually a concrete case
  where that belief gives the wrong answer.
- Split facts into must-know and cut. Keep the cut list in the brief, not on screen; every
  extra element competes with the idea.
- Show a concrete instance before the general rule. Use a metaphor only when it maps the
  mechanism more accurately than the real thing; otherwise show the real thing.
- Reorder the source material into a teaching sequence. Following the article's paragraph
  order is the common failure.

## Arc

Use this shape unless the brief or owner sets another. Proportions are a guide, not a timer.

1. **Question** (about the first tenth): open on the case people get wrong, as an image, not
   a title card. Frame 0 is the poster in feeds: show the opening image there, never black or
   a logo alone, and pose the question within the first 3 s.
2. **Model** (to about a third): build the smallest correct picture of how it works, one
   element per beat.
3. **Proof** (to about three quarters): run the model on a real case so cause and effect play
   out on screen.
4. **Turn**: change one variable and show what follows. Understanding should click here.
5. **Payoff**: return to the opening image, now read correctly, plus one next step.

## Scene contract additions

For each scene, add two fields to the existing contract:

- **Teaches**: the one thing this scene adds. A scene that adds nothing is cut or merged.
- **Check**: what the viewer can now say that they could not before. Use these lines for the
  muted and audio-only comprehension checks in review.

## Words, motion, and objects

- On-screen text anchors the narration; it does not repeat the full spoken line. Keep it to
  short labels and at most two lines at once. Captions carry the exact speech.
- Use one name per thing, every time, on screen and in narration.
- Every number traces to a source in the brief. Label illustrative values as examples.
- Motion must carry meaning: grouping, order, cause, or scale. One focal action at a time;
  everything else holds still while text is read.
- Keep objects alive across scenes and transform them rather than replacing them, so a dot can
  become a node and a label can become an axis. Track each persistent object in the storyboard.
- Reframe between ideas, not during reading. Settle without bounce unless the owner's motion
  language says otherwise.
- Prefer real UI, real data, and plain diagrams to decoration. Drop effects that do not serve
  the idea: glow, stock 3D shapes, gradient title cards, ambient particles, invented metrics.

## Choosing a direction

For a new film with no settled look, anchor to the owner's brand, product, and real screens
before drawing. Compare a few directions as real stills of the same key scene, side by side,
and record the choice and why in the film's existing brief or decision record. Pick and
continue unless the user asked to choose; an established look needs no exploration pass.
